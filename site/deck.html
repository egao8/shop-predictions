How the model works

Five steps, all using only information available at the moment the signal fires

1 · SenseHourly title odds for every team, Polymarket + Kalshi (as a cross-check), 4 leagues × 2 seasons780 candidate moves
2 · FilterMove must hold 6 hours, Kalshi must agree, enough trades, not driven by one wallet546 kept
3 · ClassifyWhich game or news caused it? Clinch, win, loss, elimination, title, rival's game, off-court news8 event types
4 · ForecastExpected 14-day demand for this type of event, adjusted by how far the odds moved. Trained on the other season onlyno lookahead
5 · ActOrder size (or stop) at T=0, plus a playbook per merchant typeone decision per event
Question 1

Can we tell real signals from noise? Yes

If a move is real, its size should predict how much demand changes. If it's noise, it shouldn't.

LeagueMoves → keptKept: size predicts demand (t)Rejected (t)
NFL138 → 115+10.5+2.4
MLB217 → 126+6.3+5.8
NBA147 → 126+2.8-0.4
NHL278 → 179+0.9+1.6

t above ~2 = a real relationship. Kept moves pass in the NFL, MLB and NBA. Rejected moves are ~0 in the NBA (as they should be), but in thin MLB markets our filter also threw out some real moves: thresholds need tuning per market. NHL: too little demand data.

Example of noise caught

October 2024, before the season: one wallet pushed the Pacers' title odds from 1.6% to 6%. No game, no news. A naive "odds spiked" alert would have told Indiana merchants to stock up seven months early. Our filter rejected it, and fan demand never moved.

Question 2

Does demand actually move after a signal? Yes, and the type of event matters more than the arrow

Demand over the next 14 days vs the week before the signal.

Direction test (does demand follow the odds?): p = <0.001 NFL, <0.001 MLB, 0.002 NBA.

The trap a raw odds alert falls into: a playoff loss drops a team's title odds, but demand doesn't fall while the team is still playing. Only eliminations collapse demand. That's why we classify every move before acting.

Question 4 · the reframe

Is the market's number better than a smart merchant's gut? Only in the NFL

Tipoff's share of the max possible profit, by league. NHL: too few events with usable data (US merch searches are sparse).

Overall: Tipoff +16% vs smart merchant +13% (p = 0.210): not a reliable edge.

NFL: Tipoff +37% vs +18% (p = 0.001), ahead in all 6 robustness variants (+11 to +22 points).

Why it might be the NFL: 17 games, so every result moves the season; one decision a week; the biggest merch market. This was not a pre-registered hypothesis, so it needs confirming on the live 2026 season.

Where the money is

"Stop" and "big moves" pay most. Ordinary good news pays less.

Tipoff's share of the max possible profit, by type of event.

Stop: when a team is eliminated, demand falls about 60% within two weeks. A merchant who simply stops restocking that night captures most of the opportunity. That's the easiest win to automate and to sell.

Big moves: the market is best at telling you which news is big enough to act on.

Ordinary good news: bump sizes vary too much from event to event to size precisely from search data.

What's NOT proven yet

The gaps, in order of importance

  1. Search interest is not sales. Every demand number is Google search volume for team merch. Real Shopify orders could move every result, up or down. Biggest gap.
  2. No real merchant has acted on a signal. The merchant behaviour is simulated.
  3. Delivery delays were ignored (your simplification). Our v1 test with real lead times: signals help slow-ordering stores most and fast print-on-demand stores least.
  4. The NFL edge was found, not predicted. It needs an out-of-sample confirmation.
  5. Thin markets. NHL demand data is too sparse to score; MLB's noise filter is too strict.
  6. Small sample of rare shocks. Trades and season-ending injuries (the Luka type) are too few to size well.
What it means for the startup

The reframe is half right, and that's enough to build on

The market as a detector: works. It tells you something happened, which way it cuts, and whether it's big.

The market as a ruler for order size: works in the NFL, not (yet) in the NBA or MLB.

So the product is an act/stop radar:

  • Stop: pause ads, cancel orders, mark down the night a season ends
  • Act: alert only on big repricings
  • Size precisely where proven (NFL first); elsewhere give a direction and a range

Price on profit, not sales. Signal-following merchants sell about +11% more units, but some of that comes with leftover stock. A cut of sales would look good while costing the merchant money.

Best customers: stores that commit early. With real lead times (v1), signals added +9% profit for 14-day-lead licensed sellers and about nothing for print-on-demand.

Bigger prize later: long-lead decisions (imports, tariffs) where big repricings are the norm.
